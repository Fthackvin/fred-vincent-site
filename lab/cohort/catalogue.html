Biography · archive text

Susan Absolon I always intended to live as an artist.  Initially, I studied languages instead, and after graduating in Spanish & Latin American studies from UCL, London in 1979, I worked for several years in finance.  In my late 20s I left my job and became a self-funding art student, graduating with a First from WSCAD, Farnham in 1988, and then with a postgraduate diploma from Central St. Martin’s.  In order to support myself I worked in the art world in London for a little over 20 years.  During this time, I was also a successful freelance designer of bespoke, contemporary rugs.  When my son grew up, I was able to give up my day job and since 2011 I’ve focused on my studio practice.  I was awarded a Juliet Gomperts bursary in 2012 and have exhibited principally in national open calls, group exhibitions and open studio events. I was shortlisted for the Rye Winter Salon prize in 2018 and long listed for the CBP Prize in 2016 and 2018. Exhibitions include:  Wells Art Contemporary;  BEEP Painting Biennial (2016/18/20);  Linden Hall Studio;  'Supernature' (touring: Middlesbrough, Rye);  SFSA Painting Open;  PS Mirabel, Manchester;  Rye Winter Salon;  The London Group Open (2015/17);  Royal Academy of Arts Summer Exhibition (2015/17);  Angus-Hughes Gallery;  Painting [Now],  Studio One Gallery;  ING Discerning Eye;  The Royal West of England Academy Open;  Matthew Burrows Selects, Unit 1 Gallery/Workshop, London;  Supernature (3rd venue), Sevenoaks Kaleidoscope Gallery. September 2021

Artist statement · archive text

I make each painting up as I go along, looking for a space, mood or form that I feel excited to work with in order to take it in a specific direction. It’s a reactive process of trial and error, slippage and disruption from which different types of picture can emerge, sometimes in series that are united thematically over an extended period of time. I like the abstract and the representational to rub along together in my work, allowing latitude for more than one interpretation. Beyond process, I make paintings in order to convey something about female experience, mortality, loss and sleep, often embedding more than one of these themes concurrently in a single painting. Working with paint generates a lively inner monologue with rich associative potential. The slippery nature of both visual and verbal semantics allows me to combine, rearrange and visualise often unrelated terms inside my head. The interaction between words and an evolving painting generates an energy that allows me to take a painting in an unplanned direction to see what thing grows. It’s a way of working that helps me to shift a painting from being an aesthetically pleasing approximate thing into something articulate and authentic with its own internal logic and voice.

Artist statement · archive text

In my paintings, images based upon anatomy and the natural world are generated via an expressive and intuitive approach to mark making and a subjective interpretation of the landscape. Distanced from the desire to present an immediately recognisable view of a specific, exterior location or place in a mimetic manner, my paintings move closer to a condition that is more representative of an interior, metaphysical territory where imagination and improvisation have a great deal of autonomy and importance. Partly inspired and influenced by a range of stimuli encountered during walks in woodland, my paintings are an open microcosm where certain phenomena, memories and observations of the outside world are reflected and revealed through the layering, reassessment and amalgamation of colours, lines, and passages of paint. Ideas relating to drawing are important and a sense of dissonance and distortion also influence the creation and combination of the forms that appear in the paintings. The images in many of my works - and the visual and emotive connections they have to the landscape are held in a fluid state - poised between a sense of resolution and disintegration that conflates the prosaic with something more mysterious and coded.

Artist statement · archive text

Painting, to Karolina, is an opening, stretching beyond dimensionality and optical perception. Its process is driven by curiosity, a desire to see what is possible by negotiating new configurations of form, colour and surface. It is her attempt to generate an active space, an environment which can be perceived and responded to through our intellectual and physical faculties. It is rich, paradoxical and flexible, embracing a reality in all its multiplicity, its continuous mutation- a state of becoming and a state of exchange. Albricht is interested in the relationship of the body and the space; the way a body corresponds to the space it occupies, how it recalibrates as it moves through it. What she is searching for in painting is a kind of a gap, a dislocation- a glimpse of a possibility. The trajectory of Albricht’s current practice relies on the work being freely manipulated, moved around the studio. Her employment of collaging means that the paintings are often worked on the floor, where she can determine their composition by reconstructing it from cut outs of old paintings, rags, scraps of canvas, and studio detritus. Albricht is constantly reviewing the materials she’s using and reaching for alternative materials such as hair, volcanic rock, textile fibres etc. The use of these substances echoes her thoughts on how the body and the painting correlate. Body being the agent of painting but also the nexus of seeing, the kind of seeing that calls for the entirety of one’s senses to participate.

Artist statement · archive text

Drawing is often my point of departure, a way of collecting in a visual diary, what I see, remember and imagine as it occurs. I use a combination of these drawings to paint from, choosing those that describe conflicting ideas to create a basis for tension. Using oil paint allows me to build up multiple translucent films, I then scrub into these films exposing earlier stages of light - scumbling and scratching shapes into being. I use types of light that come from remembering: phosphenes experienced with your eyes shut, the too bright glow of LED-lit food in the early hours and the haze of a sun-baked day – are some of the experiences that make their way into my work. Using forms that describe both internal and external structures excites me. Paint helps me to solidify the amorphous in-between, something that isn’t yet formed but is itself nonetheless and proud to be so. Edges may be read as growing amoeba-like structures, describing cartoonish speech bubbles, shells, thumb prints or arteries. I find myself drawn to surfaces that show time: bleached posters in shop fronts, rough barnacled underwater things and hard-worn jeans make the basis for colours and textures I emulate when painting. Storytelling mediates my perception; I intertwine experiences from folklore and media with personal narratives and mythologies. I often turn to motifs of paired figures, pictured in various acts of togetherness. Much like the memory of an event, the moments are perceivable in a multitude of ways – the activity undecided. Figures: fight or dance, gift or scold, jump or climb, paused on thresholds in between and so allowing for both. I work to muddy the ground to depict a point of emotional ambivalence that can be accessed. I want to articulate the moment where things briefly feel fresh; made of rib or elbow and full of potential.

Artist statement · archive text

The Unchanging Traveller series began in August 2019 and is ongoing. This series of paintings is an exploration of my experiences as the passive traveller. As a regular traveller, preferring to stare out of the window, it seemed to me that I should focus on the event of the journey and all that it is possible to see even if it is in the flicker of the eye. There is a pattern here, the return train ticket is always bought in advance, requesting a window seat facing forwards, so my view is pre-determined. As a passenger I am contained stationary within a non-space. Everything I see is stationary and viewed through a window, but it is my transportation, the train which is moving. My paintings are concerned with the suspension of time, allowing the images to live longer than a momentary glance and because they are chosen they are given a new significance. I record the journeys on my Iphone and select appropriate images to explore. I work in oils and this series is painted on square panels to acknowledge the neutral – neither landscape nor portrait.

Artist statement · archive text

This body of work is the result of a process of investigation into my own studio practice, an attempt at its systematic deconstruction, and a response to the sense of moral and practical impotence felt during the pandemic. I pulled my previous works apart, both intellectually and physically, peeling the skins of paint (originally made by squeegeeing acrylic paint and gel across aluminium or glass surfaces) from their supports and categorising them according to a basic taxonomy. I wanted to look at my practice with fresh eyes, as though it were someone else’s work, to better understand it’s essential properties and constituent parts. I attended to the sides and reverse of the support gleaning the incidental and unintentional traces and evidence of my former endeavours. In short, I subjected my entire practice to a sort of forensic examination. The resulting skins, fragments and traces of paint are presented sandwiched between sheets of glass like specimens. Indeed, in an effort to capture and convey the spirit of dispassionate enquiry which informed my investigation I have presented them as ‘findings’ rather like a 19th century entomological collection. They provide a record or index of past artistic output, a ‘natural history’ of my creative self.

Artist statement · archive text

I am interested in how objects and spaces hold intimate histories, and how ancestry, kinship, upbringing and storytelling affect personal narratives in contemporary society. But coming from a family of hoarders, I am averse to accumulating ‘stuff’, and my art practice is a constant battle between my collector tendencies and my aversion to hoarding. This body of work is a response to my great-aunt Margie’s daily diary from 1949, which includes a journey from Lands End to John o’ Groats and back that Margie did alone on horseback. The only reason I have the diary is because my family can’t throw anything away, and now the diary has become an heirloom, the only window I have into Margie’s life. My original plan to recreate Margie’s journey by bicycle is on hold due to the pandemic, so I am journeying through the leaves of the diary instead, creating paintings which celebrate Margie’s heroism alongside the everyday domesticity contained in a year in the life of my great-aunt. I am scrutinising the pages of the diary, and using paint, elevating important passages, phrases and milestones throughout Margie’s year. I feel like rewriting Margie’s words is a seventy-year collaboration, and through the intense inspection of the diary I am finding similarities between myself and Margie: we relate to each other in more ways than one.
